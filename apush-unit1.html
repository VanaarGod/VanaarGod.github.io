Period 1 · 1491–1607

Contact and Colonization

Native North America · European exploration · the Columbian Exchange · early colonies

Core Frame
What Period 1 Is Really About

1491 stands in for the diverse, complex Indigenous world that existed before sustained European contact. 1607, the founding of Jamestown, marks the shift from sporadic voyages to durable English colonization. Everything between those markers is really one long story: contact reorganizes power, land, labor, and culture on every side of the Atlantic.

Remember that Columbus was not the first European in North America — the Norse reached modern Canada around the year 1000. What makes 1492 transformative is that it starts sustained, large-scale Atlantic contact, not first contact.

  • How Jamestown and early New England set the patterns for English colonization.

Common Mistakes

Avoid These

  • Treating Native societies as one culture instead of hundreds of distinct nations.
  • Saying "Europeans wanted to explore" without naming a specific motive or actor.
  • Listing Columbian Exchange items without explaining their impact.
  • Assuming slavery began only in the English colonies rather than earlier under Spain.
Study Strategy

Best Order To Review

  • Start with Native diversity and regional adaptation.
  • Move to European motives, technology, and Columbus.
  • Study the Columbian Exchange as cause and effect.
  • Compare Spanish, French, Dutch, and English colonial models.
  • Finish with labor systems, cultural encounters, and Jamestown.

Major Buckets
01 · Peopling of the Americas

Native Nations Before Sustained Contact

The traditional explanation for how humans first reached the Americas is the Bering Land Bridge: during a colder global climate, lower sea levels exposed a land connection between Siberia and Alaska. As the planet warmed, rising seas submerged that land and formed the Bering Strait.

By 1491 there was no single Indigenous experience — North America held hundreds of distinct nations, cities, and societies. The clearest way to explain that diversity for AP writing is a causal chain: climate and geography shape available food sources, food systems shape population density, and population density shapes political organization, belief systems, and trade.

Southwest
Pueblo peoples built settled communities around maize, bean, and squash agriculture supported by irrigation in an arid climate.
Pacific Northwest
Abundant salmon supported large, permanent, socially complex settlements without large-scale farming; canoes and totem poles reflect that stability.
Great Basin
Dispersed resources favored smaller, more mobile hunting-and-gathering groups with less centralized political organization.
Northeast
Groups mixed farming, hunting, and fishing; the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) shows sophisticated multi-nation diplomacy.

AP Move

When a prompt names a region, connect environment to food system to political structure — that three-step chain earns reasoning credit on its own.
02 · Why Europe Looked Outward

European Expansion and the Competitive Atlantic

European exploration accelerated in the 1400s and 1500s for overlapping reasons. Economically, states wanted direct access to Asian trade and, after 1492, American silver, gold, sugar, and land. Religiously, many Europeans felt a duty to spread Christianity, which also supported political control. Politically, exploration became a tool of rivalry between competing monarchies.

Better ships (the caravel), instruments (the compass and astrolabe), and mapmaking made ocean crossings more reliable. In August 1492, Columbus sailed with three caravels funded by the Spanish Crown, reached the Caribbean, and encountered the Taíno — a voyage often summarized as a search for "gold, glory, and God."

Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)
Spain and Portugal, with papal support, drew a line dividing Atlantic claims — evidence that Europeans partitioned the Americas without Indigenous consent.
Joint-Stock Companies
Shareholder-funded ventures such as the Virginia Company spread the financial risk of expensive, uncertain overseas colonization.
03 · Biological Transformation

The Columbian Exchange

The Columbian Exchange was the rapid transfer of plants, animals, people, and disease across the Atlantic after 1492. It was not simple, equal trade — it remade ecosystems, economies, and populations, often through conquest and coercion.

The most consequential transfer was smallpox and other Old World diseases. With little immunity, Indigenous communities suffered catastrophic mortality that weakened resistance to conquest and destabilized leadership — largely an unintended consequence of contact with enormous political effects.

Old World → New World
Horses, pigs, cattle, wheat, and grapes reshaped land use, mobility, and warfare, especially on the Plains once horse culture spread.
New World → Old World
Corn, potatoes, tomatoes, and sweet potatoes boosted food production and population growth across Europe, Africa, and Asia.
Silver
Spanish silver extraction linked the Americas, Europe, and Asia into a more interconnected global trade network.
04 · Conquest, Labor, Religion

Spanish Conquest and Colonization

Spanish success was multi-causal, not just superior weapons. Hernán Cortés began his campaign against the Aztec Empire in 1519 by allying with rivals of the Aztecs; Francisco Pizarro conquered the Inca beginning in 1532 amid internal political division. In both cases, disease weakened resistance and destabilized rule.

Under the encomienda system, the crown granted colonists authority over Native people, who were supposedly protected and converted in exchange for forced labor and tribute — in practice often as brutal as slavery. Spain also used missions to convert Native peoples, suppressing Indigenous religion in the process. Critics such as Bartolomé de las Casas condemned the brutality, even as belief in European superiority remained nearly universal.

St. Augustine (1565)
Spanish Florida settlement; the oldest continuously occupied European-founded settlement in the present-day United States.
Ponce de León & de Soto
Explored Florida (c. 1513) and the Southeast (from 1539), extending Spanish claims north of Mexico.
05 · Competing Colonial Models

France, the Netherlands, and England

Different goals produced different colonization patterns. France emphasized the fur trade; because trade depended on Native cooperation, the French favored diplomacy and alliances, stayed few in number, and often intermarried with Native communities (the coureurs du bois). France founded Quebec City in 1608. The Dutch focused on commerce and trading posts rather than mass settlement.

England leaned toward settler colonization — migrants wanted permanent land to farm and pass on, which increased land pressure and conflict with Native peoples. England's early attempts were fragile: the 1587 Roanoke colony had vanished by 1590, and a durable settlement did not exist until Jamestown in 1607.

Spain
Conquest, missions, and coerced labor produced large-scale disruption of Native societies.
France
Trade-driven alliances created more diplomatic interdependence, with conflict tied to trade competition.
Netherlands
Commercial ports and shipping shaped a competition- and trade-centered presence.
England
Permanent agricultural settlement produced high land pressure, displacement, and frequent conflict.

AP Move

Avoid absolutes — France was not always peaceful and England was not always violent. Anchor comparisons to a clear criterion: labor system, land use, or religion.
06 · From Coercion to the Slave Trade

Labor, Slavery, and Caste in the Atlantic World

Colonizers needed large labor forces to extract wealth and grow cash crops. Indigenous labor was targeted first through systems like encomienda, but demographic collapse, harsh conditions, and resistance made it hard to sustain at scale. Enslaved Africans first arrived in Spanish colonies as early as 1501, and the labor crisis helped drive the growth of the transatlantic slave trade.

Planters increasingly preferred enslaved Africans over enslaved Native people: Africans did not know the local land and were less likely to escape, were removed from their home communities, and English colonists rationalized enslavement using constructed ideas about race. Before slavery fully dominated, indentured servitude — a set term of labor in exchange for passage — was the major Chesapeake labor system; more than 75% of the roughly 130,000 English migrants to the Chesapeake in the 1600s arrived as indentured servants.

Middle Passage
The brutal Atlantic crossing for enslaved Africans; roughly one-fifth of those aboard often died during the voyage.
Regional Slavery
Labor-intensive tobacco, rice, and indigo drove Southern plantation slavery; Northern slavery was smaller-scale, tied to farms, shipping, and domestic service.
Ending the Trade
Congress ended U.S. participation in the Atlantic slave trade in 1808 — but slavery itself continued until 1865.
07 · Cooperation, Conflict, and Blending

Cultural Encounters

Contact was an ongoing negotiation, not one moment — both sides adapted. Native peoples generally viewed land as the source of life rather than private property, while Europeans believed land should be tamed and privately owned; those mismatched assumptions fueled repeated conflict over land, labor, and religion.

Syncretism — the blending of religious or cultural traditions — appears throughout Period 1, from Native adaptations of Christian ritual to enslaved Africans blending West African traditions with Christianity, as in religions like voodoo. Some enslaved people escaped to form independent Maroon communities, an early form of resistance within the Atlantic system.

08 · English Colonization Takes Hold

Jamestown and Early New England

Jamestown (1607), funded by the Virginia Company, nearly failed — within three months, more than half the original settlers were dead from starvation or disease. John Rolfe helped save the colony by marrying Pocahontas and pioneering large-scale tobacco cultivation, a labor- and land-hungry cash crop that pushed colonists to expand and rely on coerced labor.

Virginia introduced the headright system (1618, roughly 50 acres per settler) to attract labor, and in 1619 created the House of Burgesses, the first representative assembly in English North America. That same year is also commonly used to mark the start of slavery in the English colonies. Further north, Separatists founded Plymouth in 1620 and signed the Mayflower Compact; Puritans founded Massachusetts Bay during the Great Migration (1629–1642) under Governor John Winthrop's "city upon a hill."

Chesapeake
Profit-driven, dispersed tobacco plantations, mostly single male migrants, and growing reliance on indentured and enslaved labor.
New England
Family migration, longer life expectancy, tighter town-and-meetinghouse communities, and far less reliance on enslaved labor.
Dissent
Roger Williams (banished, founded Rhode Island) and Anne Hutchinson (banished for challenging clergy authority) show Puritan intolerance of internal dissent.

AP Move

Use the Chesapeake–New England contrast as ready-made comparison evidence for any LEQ about regional divergence in colonial society.
Before 1491

Native Nations and Regional Adaptation

Environment shapes food systems, food systems shape society

Southwest
Irrigated Maize Communities

Pueblo peoples built settled towns supported by maize, bean, and squash agriculture and careful irrigation in an arid climate — a strong example of adaptation over "primitive" stereotypes.

Pacific Northwest
Abundance Without Farming

Coastal groups relied on salmon and other fish to support large, permanent, socially stratified settlements — proof that dense, complex society does not require large-scale agriculture.

Great Basin
Mobility Over Density

Dispersed resources meant smaller, more mobile groups relying on hunting and gathering, with less centralized political organization than farming societies.

Great Plains
Seasonal Movement

Before widespread horse culture intensified mobility after contact, many Plains groups combined hunting with limited farming and moved with the seasons.

Northeast
Confederated Diplomacy

Groups mixed farming, hunting, and fishing. The Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) united multiple nations to manage diplomacy and conflict — sophisticated political organization long before contact.

Southeast & Mississippi Valley
Mound-Building and Trade

Earlier mound-building cultures such as Cahokia reveal deep histories of large settlements and long-distance trade in shells, copper, obsidian, and maize well before 1491.

1400s–1500s

Why Europe Looked Outward

Economic, religious, and political motives — plus the tools that made it possible

Economic

Trade, Wealth, and New Land

Europeans wanted direct access to Asian goods and, after 1492, American silver, gold, sugar, and new commodities — plus opportunities for investment and land ownership.

Religious

Christianity and Empire

Many Europeans believed they had a duty to spread Christianity. Missionary sincerity and political control were often intertwined, not separate motives.

Political

Rivalry Between States

Exploration became a tool of competition between centralizing monarchies. Spain and Portugal led early; France, the Netherlands, and England followed to avoid falling behind.

Technology

What Made Voyages Possible

  • The caravel improved ship design for ocean travel.
  • The compass and astrolabe improved navigation and route-finding.
  • Better mapmaking and Atlantic wind/current knowledge reduced risk.
1492

Columbus and the Spanish Lead

Columbus sailed with three Spanish-funded caravels seeking a westward route to India, reached the Caribbean, and encountered the Taíno — opening sustained Atlantic contact, often summarized as a search for "gold, glory, and God."

1494

Treaty of Tordesillas

Spain and Portugal, with papal backing, drew a line dividing Atlantic claims — a reminder that empires partitioned the Americas through European diplomacy, without Indigenous consent.

After 1492

The Columbian Exchange

Biological transfer becomes economic and demographic transformation

Old World → New World
Horses, Livestock, Grain

Horses, pigs, cattle, wheat, and grapes reshaped mobility, hunting, warfare, and farming across the Americas — horses especially transformed Plains life over time.

New World → Old World
Corn, Potatoes, Tomatoes

New staple crops raised food production and helped fuel population growth across Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Catastrophe
Smallpox and Demographic Collapse

Old World diseases devastated Indigenous populations with little immunity, weakening resistance and destabilizing leadership — a largely unintended consequence with enormous political effects.

Global Trade
Silver and Interconnection

Spanish silver extraction linked the Americas, Europe, and Asia into a genuinely global trade network — evidence the Exchange was economic as much as biological.

Comparing Empires

Spain, France, the Netherlands, England

Different goals produced different colonial methods

Wealth & Conversion

Spain

Conquest, formal colonies, missions, and coerced labor (encomienda) drove large-scale disruption of Native societies, alongside forced religious conversion.

Trade & Alliance

France

Fur-trade dependence on Native cooperation encouraged diplomacy and intermarriage; fewer settlers meant more diplomatic interdependence, with conflict tied to trade competition.

Commerce & Ports

Netherlands

A commercial, shipping-focused approach favored trading posts over mass settlement, shaping competition and alliances around trade routes.

Land & Settlement

England

Settler colonization sought permanent farmland for families, producing high land pressure, displacement, and frequent conflict with Native nations.

Native View of Land & Religion

  • Land as the source of life, not a commodity to sell.
  • Often a spirit-filled natural world; some belief in one supreme being.
  • Kinship extended beyond the nuclear family to shape social organization.

European View of Land & Religion

  • Land tamed and held through private ownership.
  • The Roman Catholic Church held major political and spiritual authority.
  • Family life centered more narrowly on parents and children.
Coercion and Commerce

Labor, Slavery, and Caste

From encomienda to indentured servitude to the transatlantic slave trade

Spanish America

Encomienda

Colonists received authority over Native people, owing them protection and conversion in exchange for labor and tribute — legally distinct from slavery but frequently just as brutal in practice.

English Chesapeake

Indentured Servitude

Servants labored for a set term in exchange for passage. Conditions were harsh, but survivors could gain land and political rights — over 75% of 1600s Chesapeake migrants arrived this way.

Atlantic System

The Middle Passage

The brutal ocean crossing for enslaved Africans, the middle leg of the triangular trade. Roughly one-fifth of those aboard often died during the voyage.

Why the Shift

Africans Over Native Labor

Native people knew the land and could escape more easily; disease decimated Indigenous populations by as much as 85–95% in some regions; Africans were removed from familiar territory and networks.

Ongoing Negotiation

Cultural Encounters

Cooperation, conflict, and syncretism on both sides of contact

Cooperation

Trade, Alliance, Exchange

Europeans depended on Native knowledge, food, and guidance; both sides formed alliances against rivals. Cooperation was rarely equal — Europeans usually sought long-term dominance.

Conflict

Land, Labor, Religion

Land hunger, labor demands, forced conversion, and retaliatory cycles of violence drove recurring conflict — Indigenous resistance to colonization is a constant Period 1 theme.

Syncretism

Blending Under Pressure

Native and African peoples blended their own traditions with Christianity — as in religions like voodoo — often reshaping imposed practices rather than simply accepting them.

Resistance

Maroon Communities

Some enslaved people escaped to form independent Maroon communities, preserving cultural practices and resisting the Atlantic slave system directly.

1607 and Beyond

Jamestown and Early English Colonies

Virginia Company · headright · Burgesses · Chesapeake vs. New England

Founding
A Business Venture

The Virginia Company founded Jamestown seeking profit. Early settlers, many ill-suited gentlemen searching for gold, faced starvation and disease — more than half died within three months.

Turning Point
Rolfe and Tobacco

John Rolfe married Pocahontas, easing tensions, and pioneered large-scale tobacco cultivation — a land- and labor-hungry cash crop that pushed colonists to expand and rely on coerced labor.

1618 & 1619
Headright and Burgesses

The headright system granted roughly 50 acres to attract settlers. In 1619 Virginia created the House of Burgesses, the first representative assembly in English North America.

Further North
Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay

Separatists founded Plymouth in 1620 under the Mayflower Compact; Puritans arrived during the Great Migration (1629–1642), led by John Winthrop's vision of a "city upon a hill."

Chesapeake Pattern

  • Mostly single male migrants seeking profit.
  • Dispersed tobacco plantations, not towns.
  • Growing reliance on indentured and enslaved labor.

New England Pattern

  • Whole families migrated together.
  • Tighter town-and-meetinghouse communities.
  • Far less reliance on enslaved labor than the South.

AP Writing

How Period 1 Appears in SAQs, LEQs, and DBQs

Use evidence to support reasoning, not to decorate an answer

Use This Pattern

Evidence + Reasoning

Claim: European empires pursued different colonial strategies based on their economic goals.

Evidence: Spain built missions and encomienda around conquest; France built alliances around the fur trade.

Reasoning: Because trade required Native cooperation, France had stronger incentives to maintain diplomacy than Spain did.

Typical Prompt Move

Explain Cause and Effect

High-scoring answers link a specific cause to a specific effect — for example, tobacco's demand for land and labor directly pushed expansion and coerced labor systems in the Chesapeake.

What To Avoid

Listing Without Linking

Do not list Columbian Exchange items or European motives without explaining impact. The prompt usually wants causation, comparison, or continuity and change — not a list.

DBQ Sourcing

Point of View, Purpose, Audience

A Spanish priest describing Native labor should be read with attention to religious goals and imperial politics — sourcing earns points that summary alone cannot.
