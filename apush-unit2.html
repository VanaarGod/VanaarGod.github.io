Period 2 · 1607–1754

Colonial Society and Empire

Regional development · Atlantic economy · slavery's expansion · self-government · religious revival

Core Frame
What Period 2 Is Really About

1607, the founding of Jamestown, opens more than a century of English settlement that produced sharply different regional societies rather than one uniform "colonial America." 1754, the start of the French and Indian War, closes the period because that war reshapes British imperial policy and sets up the tensions of Period 3.

Treat colonization as negotiation among competing empires, Native nations, and colonists with different goals — not a simple story of "settlers arrive and build colonies." Geography, founding purpose, and labor demand explain why New England, the Chesapeake, the Middle Colonies, and the Lower South diverged so sharply.

Fast Review
What You Must Be Able To Explain
  • Why New England, the Chesapeake, the Middle Colonies, and the Lower South developed such different economies, labor systems, and societies.
  • How and why chattel slavery expanded and hardened into a racial, hereditary system.
  • How mercantilism and the Navigation Acts shaped — and how salutary neglect loosened — British control of the colonies.
  • How the First Great Awakening and Enlightenment ideas challenged traditional authority.
  • How events like Bacon's Rebellion, the Pueblo Revolt, and King Philip's War reveal frontier instability and Native agency.
  • Why 1754 marks a turning point in imperial and colonial relations.

Common Mistakes

Avoid These

  • Treating "the colonies" as a single, uniform society instead of distinct regions.
  • Calling New England town meetings or Virginia's Burgesses fully "democratic" without noting who was excluded.
  • Describing the shift to slavery as sudden rather than gradual across the late 1600s and early 1700s.
  • Saying the Great Awakening "caused the Revolution" instead of describing it as one contributing factor.
Study Strategy

Best Order To Review

  • Start with the Chesapeake and New England as a direct regional contrast.
  • Add the Middle Colonies and Lower South to complete the regional picture.
  • Study slavery and the Atlantic economy as one interconnected system.
  • Cover imperial policy — mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, salutary neglect.
  • Finish with religion, ideas, and the colonial wars leading to 1754.

Major Buckets
01 · A Contested Continent

Competing Empires and Native Power

Period 2 is not a simple story of "settlers arrive and build colonies." It was a long contest among Spanish, French, Dutch, and English empires, shaped at every turn by Native American power, geography, and Atlantic economics. Each empire pursued overlapping goals — wealth, territorial power, and religion or culture — but built very different colonial systems from those shared motives.

Native nations were never a passive backdrop. Disease (especially smallpox) destabilized communities and shifted regional power balances; access to European metal tools and weapons could empower some nations over rivals; and most conflicts were ultimately about land, since English settlers wanted permanent farms while many Native groups understood land as collectively used. Borderlands — regions no single group fully controlled — became spaces of trade, blending, and violence.

Spain
Extended a conquest-and-mission model from the Caribbean and Mexico into North American borderlands, often defensive and religious, backed by forced labor.
France
Prioritized the fur trade, which meant prioritizing alliances with Native nations over dense settlement.
Netherlands
Built New Netherland as a commerce-first colony centered on ports and trade.
England
Developed a patchwork of profit-driven, religious, and proprietary colonies with significant regional diversity.

AP Move

Use the Pueblo Revolt (1680) — in which Pueblo communities drove Spanish colonizers out of Santa Fe for over a decade — as concrete evidence that colonization could be reversed, at least temporarily, through organized Indigenous action.
02 · Tobacco and Plantation Society

The Chesapeake: Tobacco, Labor, and Bacon's Rebellion

Jamestown (1607) was founded by the Virginia Company as a profit-seeking venture, not a religious refuge, and struggled early due to conflict with the Powhatan Confederacy, disease, and unsafe water. Virginia stabilized once John Rolfe developed a marketable tobacco strain. Tobacco depleted soil quickly and demanded land, creating a feedback loop: profits drew more migrants, migrants demanded more land, expansion increased Native conflict, and conflict required militia coordination.

Early Chesapeake labor relied on indentured servants who traded a term of labor for passage. Freed servants who competed for scarce coastal land and pushed onto the frontier helped set up Bacon's Rebellion (1676): frontier farmers, led by Nathaniel Bacon, wanted permission to attack nearby Native groups after Governor William Berkeley hesitated. Bacon's forces attacked the Susquehannock and Pamunkey — English allies — then sacked and burned Jamestown itself before Bacon died of dysentery. The rebellion exposed deep class conflict among colonists and pushed elites toward a more controllable labor force: race-based, hereditary chattel slavery, which expanded gradually rather than overnight.

Maryland
A proprietary colony under Cecilius Calvert (Lord Baltimore), intended as a Catholic haven; the Act of Toleration (1649) protected Christian worship but tensions persisted.
House of Burgesses (1619)
The first representative assembly in English North America — but political participation stayed sharply limited by property, gender, race, and status.
Elite Response
Frontier insecurity and class resentment convinced planters that enslaved labor, bound for life, was safer than a growing population of angry landless freedmen.

AP Move

Explain Bacon's Rebellion through mechanism — frontier insecurity, unequal land access, and elite resentment — not just the date, and treat the shift to slavery as gradual, not sudden.
03 · Towns, Church, and Dissent

New England: Puritan Migration and Community Governance

Puritans sought to "purify" the Church of England and migrated in large numbers during the Great Migration of the 1630s, founding Massachusetts Bay under John Winthrop's vision of a "city upon a hill." Poor Chesapeake-style soil discouraged plantation agriculture, so New England's economy centered on small farms, fishing, shipbuilding, and trade. Whole families migrated together, supporting longer life expectancy and more stable, tightly knit town-and-meetinghouse communities than the Chesapeake.

Political rights were often tied to church membership, and dissenters were banished — Roger Williams (who argued for separating church and state) founded Rhode Island, and Anne Hutchinson was banished for challenging clergy authority. Expansion produced the Pequot War and the devastating King Philip's War (1675–1676), led by Metacom. As religious fervor cooled across generations, the Halfway Covenant (1662) allowed baptism for children of baptized-but-unconverted parents, though full political participation still required full church membership.

Salem Witch Trials (1692)
More than 130 accused witches were jailed or executed amid fear tied to the Dominion of New England's collapse, a new royal charter, and frontier war anxiety.
Fundamental Orders of Connecticut
Founded with Thomas Hooker's help, sometimes cited as the first written constitution in British North America.
By 1700
Women made up the majority of active church members, reflecting shifting religious participation as fervor declined among some men.

AP Move

Never call New England simply "democratic" — town meetings were real participation, but rights were tied to church membership, gender, and race.
04 · Diversity and Commerce

The Middle Colonies

The Middle Colonies — New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware — are best understood through diversity and their role as an Atlantic crossroads. New York began as the Dutch colony of New Netherland; Charles II seized it in 1664 and gave it to his brother James, Duke of York. Dutch residents stayed on generous terms, helping explain New York's lasting commercial character.

Pennsylvania, founded by William Penn as a refuge for Quakers, promoted religious freedom and grew rapidly through active advertising, drawing large numbers of German and Scots-Irish settlers alongside the English. Fertile soil earned the region the nickname the "bread colonies" for large-scale grain production, and port cities like Philadelphia and New York became hubs for shipping, artisan labor, and trade — supporting a comparatively large middle class.

Penn's Treaty
Penn agreed to take only as much Delaware land as could be walked in three days; his son later hired marathon runners to claim far more — a lesson in how "treaties" could be manipulated.
Quaker Beliefs
Emphasized the "inner light," spiritual equality, and pacifism — ideals that shaped Pennsylvania's relatively tolerant reputation.

AP Move

Don't equate "diverse" with "equal" — explain diversity's causes (immigration, trade, founding purpose) and its real limits.
05 · Rice, Slavery, and Buffer Colonies

The Lower South: Carolinas and Georgia

Carolina began as a proprietary colony and split into North and South Carolina in 1729. South Carolina was settled largely by planters from Barbados, who brought Caribbean sugar-plantation expectations and helped accelerate mainland slavery; the colony soon had a larger enslaved than free population, making it one of the most slavery-dependent colonies in British North America.

Georgia (1732), founded under James Oglethorpe partly as a military buffer against Spanish Florida, initially banned slavery — but the ban was overturned once neighboring South Carolina's growth underscored slavery's profitability. Over time, Britain converted many proprietary colonies into royal colonies to increase crown control; by the Revolution, only Connecticut, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, and Maryland remained non-royal.

Rice & Indigo
Rice cultivation especially benefited from enslaved Africans' existing agricultural knowledge, deepening the region's reliance on forced labor.
North vs. South Carolina
North Carolina drew settlers largely from Virginia; South Carolina's Barbadian roots pushed it toward plantation slavery much faster.

AP Move

Distinguish Chesapeake tobacco from Lower South rice and indigo plantations — "the South" was not one uniform system.
06 · Coercion and the Atlantic Economy

Slavery and the Atlantic World

The Atlantic world connected Europe, Africa, and the Americas through migration, trade, and empire. Colonies exported tobacco, rice, indigo, fish, and lumber; Europe supplied manufactured goods; and enslaved Africans were forcibly transported to supply labor. "Triangular trade" is best treated as a simplified model of overlapping routes, not one rigid path — the key idea is interdependence across the ocean.

Slavery expanded because staple-crop agriculture demanded labor, indentured servitude declined after unrest like Bacon's Rebellion, and enslaved labor generated major profits. Colonies gradually built race-based, hereditary chattel slavery through law and culture, defining enslaved people as property, making status pass through the mother, and restricting movement and assembly. The Stono Rebellion (1739), near Charleston, South Carolina, was one of the most significant slave uprisings in British North America — roughly 20 enslaved people seized weapons and tried to reach Spanish Florida before a militia stopped them; the aftermath brought harsher slave codes across the colonies.

Regional Variation
Tied to tobacco in the Chesapeake, to rice and indigo in the Lower South, and smaller-scale in New England — though Northern merchants profited heavily from Atlantic commerce tied to slavery.
Resistance
Ranged from daily acts like work slowdowns and cultural preservation to escape and organized rebellion such as Stono.

AP Move

Treat slavery as more than a labor supply — connect it to politics (elite power), social order (racial hierarchy), and culture (fear of revolt, surveillance).
07 · Regulation and Autonomy

Imperial Policy and Colonial Self-Government

Mercantilism — the era's dominant economic theory — held that a nation's power depended on a favorable balance of trade, so colonies existed to enrich the mother country. Britain enforced this through the Navigation Acts (1651–1673), which required colonists to trade mainly through England, plus laws like the Wool Act (1699) and the widely evaded Molasses Act (1733).

In practice, Britain often used absentee customs officials and inconsistent enforcement — a pattern historians call salutary (benign) neglect — which let colonists smuggle, bribe, or ignore rules and encouraged real self-government. Each colony had a royal-or-proprietor-appointed governor who nonetheless depended on elected legislatures for money; most colonies had bicameral legislatures, with a property-restricted lower house holding the "power of the purse." When Britain did try to tighten control through the Dominion of New England (1686–1689), colonists resisted, and the Dominion collapsed after the Glorious Revolution.

New England Confederation
A limited regional coordination effort with little real power, but a model for intercolonial cooperation.
West Indies Priority
Britain often valued Caribbean sugar colonies more than mainland North America because of their enormous profits.

AP Move

Link mercantilist theory to specific laws (Navigation Acts, Molasses Act) and then to the effect of weak enforcement: growing colonial autonomy and political expectations.
08 · Revival, Reason, and Rivalry

Religion, Ideas, and Wars for Empire

The First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) was a wave of religious revival emphasizing emotional, personal conversion over inherited church authority. Jonathan Edwards preached severe Calvinist doctrine, while George Whitefield drew huge crowds with emotionally intense preaching, splitting communities into New Lights and Old Lights. By encouraging colonists to judge their own spiritual state, the Awakening indirectly encouraged questioning of other hierarchies — without producing full social equality. Enlightenment ideas about reason and natural law circulated alongside revival religion; Benjamin Franklin, with inventions like bifocals and the lightning rod and civic projects like a library and fire department, embodied Enlightenment practicality.

Meanwhile, recurring wars — King William's War, Queen Anne's War, and King George's War — showed that European rivalries regularly spilled into North America, with Native nations as decisive, independent actors rather than mere "helpers." 1754 marks the start of the French and Indian War (part of the global Seven Years' War) and the end of Period 2, because that conflict reshapes British imperial policy going forward.

Why Intercolonial Unity Was Hard
Regional economic differences, local political priorities, and competition for land and trade slowed cooperation before 1754.
Native Diplomacy
Alliances with a European power could bring trade goods or military support, but also risked pulling Native communities into wars not their own.

AP Move

Frame colonial wars as European rivalry plus independent Native strategy, not simply "British versus French" — and use 1754 as a clean bridge into Period 3.
Regional Divergence

Four Colonial Regions, Four Societies

Geography and founding purpose shape economy, labor, and governance

Family Farms & Towns

New England

Poor soil discouraged plantations, so small farms, fishing, and shipbuilding supported tight town-and-meetinghouse communities built around Puritan congregations and family migration.

Tobacco & Plantations

The Chesapeake

Virginia and Maryland built a land- and labor-hungry tobacco economy around dispersed plantations, first worked by indentured servants and increasingly by enslaved Africans.

Grain & Ports

Middle Colonies

New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania combined fertile "bread colony" grain farming with busy port cities, drawing an ethnically and religiously diverse population.

Rice, Indigo & Slavery

The Lower South

The Carolinas and Georgia built plantation economies around rice and indigo, worked overwhelmingly by enslaved labor, especially after Barbadian planters arrived in South Carolina.

Regions by Labor System

  • New England: family labor, limited slavery.
  • Chesapeake: indentured servitude shifting toward chattel slavery.
  • Middle Colonies: mixed free and enslaved labor.
  • Lower South: heavy reliance on enslaved labor.

Regions by Governance

  • New England: town meetings, church-linked participation.
  • Chesapeake: House of Burgesses, planter-elite politics.
  • Middle Colonies: proprietary and royal mixes, notably tolerant Pennsylvania.
  • Lower South: proprietary origins converting toward royal control.
Imperial Regulation

Mercantilism and the Atlantic Economy

Why Britain regulated colonial trade — and why enforcement stayed loose

Core Theory

Mercantilism

National power depended on a favorable balance of trade and control of hard currency. Under this logic, colonies existed to enrich the mother country by supplying raw materials and buying finished goods.

1651–1673

Navigation Acts

Required colonists to trade mainly through England, restricted which goods could go where, and limited colonial manufacturing of items already produced in England.

1733

Molasses Act

Taxed sugar imported from the French West Indies at a steep rate. New Englanders widely refused to pay — an early, practical model for later resistance to Crown taxation.

The System

Triangular Trade

Best understood as overlapping Atlantic trade routes rather than one rigid triangle: colonial raw materials, European manufactured goods, and enslaved African labor all moved across the ocean in an interdependent system.

Loose Enforcement

Salutary Neglect

Britain regulated trade on paper but, using absentee customs officials, often ignored violations in practice — encouraging smuggling and real colonial self-government.

Priorities

The West Indies Mattered More

Britain frequently valued Caribbean sugar colonies above mainland North America because of their enormous profits — mainland colonies mattered mainly as markets and raw-material suppliers.

Coercion and Race

Slavery's Expansion in British North America

From indentured servitude to hereditary, race-based bondage

Origins
Present From the Start, Expanding Gradually

Slavery existed in Virginia by 1619, but it expanded gradually. Settlers from Barbados brought a working model of large-scale plantation slavery that accelerated its spread on the mainland.

Legal Construction
Chattel Slavery Hardens Into Law

Colonies gradually defined enslaved people as property for life, made status hereditary through the mother, and restricted movement and assembly — building a racial hierarchy separating white freedom from Black enslavement.

Not Identical Everywhere
Regional Variation

Tobacco tied slavery to the Chesapeake; rice and indigo tied it to the Lower South; New England's enslaved population stayed smaller, though Northern merchants profited from Atlantic slave commerce.

1739
The Stono Rebellion

Roughly 20 enslaved people near Charleston seized weapons and tried to reach Spanish Florida before a militia stopped them. The aftermath brought harsher slave codes and heightened colonial fear of revolt.

1700–1750

Colonial Society: Growth, Gender, and City Life

The colonies stop looking like "transplanted England"

1700 → 1750

Rapid Population Growth

Colonial population rose from about 250,000 to about 1,250,000, fueled by continued English migration plus large numbers of Scots-Irish, Scots, and Germans; the Black population passed 200,000 by 1750.

Rural Life

Patriarchy and Limited Rights

Over 90% of colonists lived in rural areas under patriarchal norms. Women generally could not vote, draft a will, or testify in court, and labor was divided sharply along gender lines.

Regional Variation

Black Life and Community

Most Black colonists lived in the rural South, where enslaved people built extended kinship networks as a survival strategy; smaller Northern populations made sustained community-building harder.

Cities & Colleges

Opportunity and Hardship

Cities offered wider contact and education but also poverty and epidemics like smallpox. Harvard (1636), the College of William and Mary (1693), and Yale (1701) mainly trained ministers.

Rural Experience

  • Subsistence farming with gendered division of labor.
  • Limited social contact outside the family unit.
  • Legal and political rights concentrated in propertied men.

Urban Experience

  • Wages often too low to escape poverty.
  • Primitive sanitation and recurring epidemics.
  • Greater exposure to ideas, print culture, and social change.
Loose Reins

Colonial Self-Government

Governors, legislatures, and the limits of imperial control

Appointed, But Dependent

Royal & Proprietary Governors

Governors held king-like powers on paper but depended on colonial legislatures for funding, which in practice forced cooperation and compromise.

Power of the Purse

Bicameral Legislatures

Most colonies (Pennsylvania was the main exception) had a property-restricted elected lower house controlling taxation, and an appointed upper house drawn from the local elite.

Limited Reach

New England Confederation

An early regional coordination effort among northeastern colonies with little real authority, but a model for intercolonial discussion of shared problems.

1686–1689

Dominion of New England

An attempt to consolidate colonies and strengthen royal authority provoked resistance and collapsed after the Glorious Revolution — evidence colonists would resist real centralization.

1730s–1740s

The Great Awakening and Enlightenment Ideas

Revival, reason, and a challenge to inherited authority

Revival

The First Great Awakening

A wave of religious revival emphasizing emotional, personal conversion over inherited church authority. Jonathan Edwards preached severe Calvinist doctrine; George Whitefield's dramatic preaching drew massive crowds across the colonies.

Community Split

New Lights vs. Old Lights

Revivalism divided congregations between supporters of emotional revival (New Lights) and defenders of traditional, educated clergy (Old Lights) — a recurring source of local conflict.

Reason Alongside Revival

The Enlightenment and Franklin

Enlightenment ideas about reason, natural law, and science circulated alongside revival religion. Benjamin Franklin embodied practical Enlightenment thought through inventions, civic projects, and later diplomacy.

Why It Mattered

Questioning Authority — With Limits

If individuals could judge their own spiritual state, they might question other hierarchies too — but the Awakening did not produce full social equality, and its effects varied widely by colony.

Frontier & Empire

Rebellions, Native Resistance, and Wars for Empire

Recurring instability on the path to 1754

1676
Bacon's Rebellion

Frontier resentment toward coastal elites and unequal land access erupted into an attack on Native allies and the burning of Jamestown, exposing deep class conflict among English colonists.

1680
Pueblo Revolt

Pueblo communities coordinated to drive Spanish colonizers out of Santa Fe for over a decade, proving colonization could be reversed, at least temporarily.

1675–1676
King Philip's War

Led by Metacom, one of the deadliest wars relative to population in colonial history, devastating Native communities and accelerating English land dispossession in New England.

1689–1748
Colonial Wars for Empire

King William's War, Queen Anne's War, and King George's War show recurring patterns: European rivalries spilling into North America, frontier raids, and shifting Native alliances built on independent strategic goals.

Causes of Frontier Conflict

  • Expansion onto contested or Native-controlled land.
  • Unequal access to coastal land, pushing settlers to the frontier.
  • Weak intercolonial coordination against shared threats.

Path to 1754

  • Repeated European wars normalize North American frontier fighting.
  • Native nations act as independent, strategic powers, not mere allies.
  • 1754 opens the French and Indian War, closing Period 2.

AP Writing

How Period 2 Appears in SAQs, LEQs, and DBQs

Use evidence to support reasoning, not to decorate an answer

Use This Pattern

Evidence + Reasoning

Claim: English colonial regions developed distinct societies because of geography and founding purpose.

Evidence: New England's poor soil produced family farms and towns; the Chesapeake's tobacco economy produced dispersed plantations reliant on coerced labor.

Reasoning: Because environment shaped what colonists could grow and how they had to organize labor, regions diverged even though they shared an English colonial framework.

Typical Prompt Move

Explain Cause and Effect

High-scoring answers link a specific cause to a specific effect — for example, Bacon's Rebellion's class conflict directly pushed Chesapeake elites toward a more controllable, permanent labor force.

What To Avoid

Listing Without Linking

Do not list the Navigation Acts or colonial wars without explaining their purpose or effect. The prompt usually wants causation, comparison, or continuity and change — not a list.

DBQ Sourcing

Point of View, Purpose, Audience

A royal customs official describing colonial smuggling should be read with attention to imperial goals and self-interest — sourcing earns points that summary alone cannot.
